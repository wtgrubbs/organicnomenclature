IUPAC Nomenclature Guide

Step 1: Identify the Parent Chain

Find the longest continuous carbon chain. The number of carbons dictates the root name:

  • 1 = meth-, 2 = eth-, 3 = prop-, 4 = but-, 5 = pent-
  • 6 = hex-, 7 = hept-, 8 = oct-, 9 = non-, 10 = dec-

Step 2: Identify the Functional Group (Suffix)

The highest priority group determines the ending of the name:

  • Alkanes: Single bonds only. Suffix: -ane (e.g., ethane)
  • Alkenes: Carbon-carbon double bond. Suffix: -ene (e.g., but-2-ene)
  • Alkynes: Carbon-carbon triple bond. Suffix: -yne (e.g., ethyne)
  • Alcohols: Contains -OH. Suffix: -ol (e.g., propan-1-ol)
  • Aldehydes: Contains -CHO at the end. Suffix: -al (e.g., ethanal)
  • Ketones: Contains C=O in the middle. Suffix: -one (e.g., propanone)
  • Carboxylic Acids: Contains -COOH at the end. Suffix: -oic acid (e.g., ethanoic acid)

Step 3: Numbering & Substituents (Prefixes)

Number the carbon chain starting from the end that gives the primary functional group the lowest number. Then, assign numbers to secondary substituents (like chloro, bromo, methyl, ethyl) and list them alphabetically at the start of the name.

Evolution of IUPAC Rules

It is important to note that IUPAC rules have evolved over time. For example, while 3-chloro-1-butene and 3-chlorobut-1-ene represent the same compound, the latter representation is considered the primary name for this compound according to current IUPAC naming conventions.

If you are interested in learning more about the comprehensive nomenclature rules for organic compounds, we highly encourage you to consult the official IUPAC Blue Book.
